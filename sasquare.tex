    \chapter*{Introduction}
    In computer science, a developer uses an array to represent a series of elements of the same data type such as integers or characters. It is a data structure that holds a list of elements; each of the elements is indexed to determine its position in the list. In mathematics, the best way to represent an array is to create a row vector, a matrix with only one row. We will use a matrix because a set is unordered and does not use indices to ordinally label its elements.

    When representing an n-by-n or a square grid using arrays, a developer will intuitively use two-dimensional arrays to represent the rows and columns. In each array that represents a row, there are arrays that represent different columns. However, we can represent a square grid with only one array that can later be translated into a two-dimensional array.

    This paper aims to illustrate the conversion of a row vector with a perfect square number of elements into a perfect square matrix or vice-versa to represent a square grid.

    \chapter{Preliminaries}
    Most of the following equations in this book are heavily reliant on the ceiling and floor functions. These two functions are used to approximate a number by an integer (\cite{iverson_notation}). We will use his notations for the ceiling function and the floor function.

    \section{The Ceiling Function}

    We will denote the application of the ceiling function on a rational number like this:
    \begin{equation}
        y = \left\lceil x \right\rceil
    \end{equation}
    Let $x$ be any rational number. The value of $y$ will be equal to $x$ only when $x$ is also an integer or a whole number, having no fractional parts or its decimal part is 0. If $x$ is a positive rational number, we will remove its fractional part or turn its decimal part into zero, then we will add 1. If $x$ is a negative rational number, we will just simply remove the fractional part or turn the decimal part into zero.

    The number 3.14 is a rational number and has a decimal part of .14, we will remove the decimal part of 3.14 and add 1 to it.
    \begin{equation*}
        y = \left\lceil 3.14 \right\rceil = 4
    \end{equation*}

    The number $-6\frac{1}{4}$ is a mixed number and has a fractional part of $\frac{1}{4}$, we will remove the fractional part of $-6\frac{1}{4}$ and do nothing else since $-6\frac{1}{4}$ is a negative number.
    \begin{equation*}
        y = \left\lceil -6\frac{1}{4} \right\rceil = -6
    \end{equation*}

    Any improper function must first be solved by dividing the numerator by the denominator. Should there be a remainder, we will increment the quotient by 1, otherwise, we leave the quotient as is.
    \begin{equation*}
        \begin{split}
            y &= \left\lceil \frac{24}{5} \right\rceil \\
            y &= \left\lceil 4\frac{4}{5} \right\rceil \\
            y &= 5
        \end{split}
    \end{equation*}

    The number $\frac{-49}{5}$ is a negative rational number. The division must be done first. We will just retain the quotient for it is a negative number; the result will be -9.
    \begin{equation*}
        \begin{split}
            y &= \left\lceil \frac{-49}{5} \right\rceil \\
            y &= \left\lceil -9\frac{4}{5} \right\rceil \\
            y &= -9
        \end{split}
    \end{equation*}

    Whether the number is positive or negative, as long as it is an integer, we will leave it as is.
    \begin{equation*}
        \begin{split}
            y &= \left\lceil 69.0 \right\rceil = 69\\
            y &= \left\lceil -69.0 \right\rceil = -69\\
        \end{split}
    \end{equation*}

    \section{The Floor Function}
    The floor function is the opposite of the ceiling function. We will denote it like this:
    \begin{equation}
        y = \left\lfloor x \right\rfloor
    \end{equation}
    Should $x$ be an integer, we will leave it as is. However, should it be a positive rational number, we will just turn its decimal part into 0 or remove the fractional part. Should it be a negative rational number, not only we will remove the fractional part but we will also subtract 1 from it.

    We simply turned the decimal part into 0 since 3.14 is a positive rational number.
    \begin{equation*}
        y = \left\lfloor 3.14 \right\rfloor = 3
    \end{equation*}

    The number $-6\frac{1}{4}$ is a negative mixed number, we removed its fractional part and subtracted 1 from -6.
    \begin{equation*}
        y = \left\lfloor -6\frac{1}{4} \right\rfloor = -7
    \end{equation*}

    Any improper function must first be solved by dividing the numerator by the denominator. Should there be a remainder, it will be just ignored and the whole part will be the result.
    \begin{equation*}
        \begin{split}
            y &= \left\lfloor \frac{24}{5} \right\rfloor \\
            y &= \left\lfloor 4\frac{4}{5} \right\rfloor \\
            y &= 4
        \end{split}
    \end{equation*}

    Should there be a remainder and the mixed number itself is negative, we will subtract 1 from the whole number as a result.
    \begin{equation*}
        \begin{split}
            y &= \left\lfloor \frac{-49}{5} \right\rfloor \\
            y &= \left\lfloor -9\frac{4}{5} \right\rfloor \\
            y &= -10
        \end{split}
    \end{equation*}

    Whether the number is positive or negative, as long as it is an integer, we will leave it as is.
    \begin{equation*}
        \begin{split}
            y &= \left\lfloor 69.0 \right\rfloor = 69\\
            y &= \left\lfloor -69.0 \right\rfloor = -69\\
        \end{split}
    \end{equation*}

    \section{Floor and Ceiling of a Zero Whole Part Number}
    The equations $\left\lceil x \right\rceil = 1$ and $\left\lfloor x \right\rfloor = 0$ are true when $0 < x < 1$ and $x \in \mathbb{Q} \setminus \mathbb{Z}$ (a rational number that has no fractional part).

    The equations $\left\lceil x \right\rceil = 0$ and $\left\lfloor x \right\rfloor = -1$ are true when $-1 < x < 0$ and $x \in \mathbb{Q} \setminus \mathbb{Z}$.
    
    \section{Division Theory and Modulo} \label{modulo}
    First of all, the divisor must not be $0$. Let $x$ be the dividend and $y$ be the divisor. Dividing $x$ and $y$ may yield a remainder, yet it will surely yield a quotient. Let $r$ be the remainder and $q$ be the quotient.
    \begin{equation}
      q = \frac{x}{y} 
    \end{equation}
But it may yield a remainder. Should it yield a remainder, we will not be dealing with just integers anymore. We want to deal with integers only, thus...
    \begin{equation}
      x = qy + r
    \end{equation}
We will be using the Modulo function. It is a function that returns the remainder of $\frac{x}{y}$. Assume the $r$ earlier is $x\bmod y$.
    \begin{equation}
      x\bmod y = x - y\left\lfloor \frac{x}{y} \right\rfloor
    \end{equation}

    \section{The Base of the Square} \label{base}
    The \textbf{base} of a square is the root of the square. We will use it in most of the following expressions to determine particular parts of a square grid. Throughout the paper, we'll represent the base as $b$. 

    Let $b^2$ be any natural number. The root of a perfect square can be positive or negative; for the sake of simplicity, we will use positive bases only. The number of elements in a matrix or an array is always an integer.

    \section{The Cells} \label{cells}
    The \textbf{cells} are the basic building blocks of a grid. Since a square matrix or grid can be thought of as a linear array, we will call the elements of an array or a matrix as cells. The number of cells in a square grid is $b^2$. We will refer to this as the \textbf{length of the array}. We shall use integers to index each cell and call each index a \textbf{cell index}.
    \begin{equation}
      (i_n)_{n=0}^{b^2-1} = (i_0, i_1, \dots ,i_{b^2-1})
    \end{equation}

    A square grid has $b$ rows and $b$ columns. We will index all rows from 1 to $b$; we will call this each index a \textbf{row index}. We will also do the same to the columns and call each index a \textbf{column index}. Usually an array is expressed as $A_{m \times n}$, but in this case, $m = n = b$.

    \section{The Rows} \label{rows}
    The \textbf{rows} are the horizontal arrangement of elements in a matrix. In a row, there are $b$ cells. We shall denote a row index as $a_{r,*}$. We shall use function $\operatorname{r}(b,n)$ to determine the row of cell index $n$ in a square grid of base $b$.
    \begin{equation}
      \operatorname{r}(b,n) = \left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor + 1
    \end{equation}
    All leftmost cells of the grid are always indexed with a number divisible by $b$. Let matrix indices $a_{r,1}$ and $a_{r,b}$ be the leftmost and rightmost cells of a row respectively.

    \section{The Columns} \label{columns}
    The \textbf{columns} are the vertical arrangement of elements in a matrix. In a column, there are $b$ cells. We shall denote a column index as $a_{*,c}$. To find the column where a cell belongs, we can simply use the modulo of $b$. We divide $n$ by $b$ and take the remainder, then add $1$.
    \begin{equation}
      \operatorname{c}(b,n) = (n\bmod b) + 1
    \end{equation}

    The first row of the square, $a_{1,*}$ has all the possible remainders of $\frac{n}{b}$ as cell indices.

    \chapter{The Intersection} \label{intersection}
    All cells belong to only one row and one column, hence $i_n$ can become $a_{r,c}$. An \textbf{intersection} is where a row and a column meet; they can only meet in one cell. The result of an intersection can be represented as either $i_n$ or $a_{r,c}$. If we can determine the row and column index of a cell index with a given base, we can also determine the cell index that results to the intersection a row and a column. To do so, we'll use the function $\operatorname{i}$. Let $r$ and $c$ be the row and column index respectively.
    \begin{equation}
        \begin{split}
          \operatorname{i}(b,r,c) &= b(r-1) + (c - 1)
        \end{split}
    \end{equation}
    In the expression, the part $b(r-1)$ denotes the rows before the row of the selected cell. The part $c - 1$ denotes the nth cell of the row. The $ -1$ in $c-1$ is simply to offset since the cell indices start at zero.

    In the only example of Chapter \ref{1_square_grid} (Section \ref{1_example_1}), we used the functions $\operatorname{r}$ and $\operatorname{c}$ to determine the matrix notation for $i_{16}$ which is $a_{4,2}$. Cell index 16 belongs to row index 4 and column index 2. We shall use function $\operatorname{i}$ to test if the intersection of $a_{4,*}$ and $a_{*,2}$ is $i_{16}$ when the $b$ is 5.
    \begin{equation*}
        \begin{split}
          i(b,r,c) &= b(r-1) + (c - 1) \\
            i(5,4,2) &= 5(4-1) + (2 - 1) \\
                &= 5(3) + 2 - 1 \\
                &= 15 + 1\\
            i(5,4,2) &= 16
        \end{split}
    \end{equation*}

    This function can be used to enumerate the cells in a chosen row or column.

    \section{Cells of a Row} \label{row_cells}
    To enumerate the cells of a row, we will use the Intersection function $\operatorname{i}$. A row shall be considered a set of cell indices. Per Chapter \ref{1_square_grid}, the number of cells in a certain row is always $b$.
    \begin{equation}
        \begin{split}
            a_{r,*}b &= \{ \operatorname{i}(b,r,c) : c = 1, \dots, b \}
        \end{split}
    \end{equation}
    The expression $a_{r,*}b$ simply means `Row $r$ of $b$-square'. Numbers $b$ and $r$ are constants inside the set builder notation.

    \section{Cells of a Column} \label{column_cells}
    To enumerate the cells of a column, we will use the Intersection function $i$. Just like a row, a column is also a set of cell indices. Just like rows, a column has only $b$ number of cells.
    \begin{equation}
        \begin{split}
            a_{*,c}b &= \{ \operatorname{i}(b,r,c) : r = 1, \dots, b \}
        \end{split}
    \end{equation}
    The expression $a_{*,c}b$ simply means `Column $c$ of $b$-square'. Numbers $b$ and $c$ are constants inside the set builder notation.

    \section{Example: The Cells of Row 4 and Column 2 in 5-square} \label{2_example_1}
    To enumerate the cell indices of $a_{4,*}5$ we shall use function $\operatorname{i}$ in a set builder notation where $r$ and $b$ is constant.
    \begin{equation*}
        \begin{split}
            a_{r,*}b &= \{ \operatorname{i}(b,r,c) : c = 1, \dots, b \} \\
            a_{4,*}5 &= \{ \operatorname{i}(5,4,c) : c = 1,2,3,4,5 \} \\
            \\
            \operatorname{i}(5,4,1) &= 5(4-1) + (1 - 1) = 15 \\
            \operatorname{i}(5,4,2) &= 5(4-1) + (2 - 1) = 16 \\
            \operatorname{i}(5,4,3) &= 5(4-1) + (3 - 1) = 17 \\
            \operatorname{i}(5,4,4) &= 5(4-1) + (4 - 1) = 18 \\
            \operatorname{i}(5,4,5) &= 5(4-1) + (5 - 1) = 19 \\
            a_{4,*}5 &= \{15, 16, 17, 18, 19 \} \\
        \end{split}
    \end{equation*}

    The set $a_{4,*}5$ has cell indices $i_{15}, i_{16}, i_{17}, i_{18}$, and $i_{19}$.

    To enumerate the cell indices of $a_{*,2}5$ we shall use function $\operatorname{i}$ in a set builder notation where $c$ and $b$ is constant.
    \begin{equation*}
        \begin{split}
            a_{*,c}b &= \{ \operatorname{i}(b,r,c) : r = 1, \dots, b \} \\
            a_{*,2}5 &= \{ \operatorname{i}(5,r,2) : r = 1,2,3,4,5 \} \\
            \\
            \operatorname{i}(5,4,1) &= 5(1-1) + (2 - 1) = 1 \\
            \operatorname{i}(5,4,2) &= 5(2-1) + (2 - 1) = 6 \\
            \operatorname{i}(5,4,3) &= 5(3-1) + (2 - 1) = 11 \\
            \operatorname{i}(5,4,4) &= 5(4-1) + (2 - 1) = 16 \\
            \operatorname{i}(5,4,5) &= 5(5-1) + (2 - 1) = 21 \\
            a_{4,*}5 &= \{1, 6, 11, 16, 21 \} \\
        \end{split}
    \end{equation*}

    The set $a_{*,2}5$ has cell indices $i_{1}, i_{6}, i_{11}, i_{16}$, and $i_{21}$.

    Set $a_{4,*}5$ and set $a_{*,2}5$, will always meet at one cell; all pairs of $a_{r,*}b$ and $a_{*,c}b$ shall always have an intersection.
    \begin{equation*}
        \begin{split}
            a_{4,*}5 &= \{15, 16, 17, 18, 19\} \\
            a_{*,2}5 &= \{1, 6, 11, 16, 21 \} \\
            a_{4,*}5 \cap a_{*,2}5 &= \{16\}
        \end{split}
    \end{equation*}

    The intersection of $a_{4,*}5$ and $a_{*,2}5$ is $i_{16}$ or $a_{4,2}$.

    \section{The Intersection Identity} \label{intersection_identity}
    The intersection function $\operatorname{i}$ needs the base, the row, and the column. What if we substitute the parameters for the row and the column? Let us see.
    \begin{equation}
      \begin{aligned}
        r(b,n) &= \left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor + 1 \\
        c(b,n) &= (n\bmod b) + 1 \\
        i(b,r,c) &= b(r-1)+(c-1) \\
        i(b,r(b,n),c(b,n)) &= b(\left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor + 1 -1)+((n\bmod b) + 1 -1) \\
        i(b,n) &= b\left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor + (n\bmod b) \\
        i(5,16) &= 5\lfloor \frac{16}{5}\rfloor + (16\bmod 5) \\
                &= 5(3) + 1 \\
                &= 15 + 1 \\
        i(5,16) &= 16 
      \end{aligned} 
    \end{equation}
    Function $c$ may also be $n - b\left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor + 1$ since it uses the modulo operator (see Section \ref{modulo}).
    \begin{equation}
      \begin{aligned}
        r(b,n) &= \left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor + 1 \\
        c(b,n) &= n - b\left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor + 1 \\
        i(b,r,c) &= b(r-1)+(c-1) \\
        i(b,r(b,n),c(b,n)) &= b(\left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor + 1 -1)+(n - b\left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor + 1 -1) \\
                &= b(\left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor)+(n - b\left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor) \\
                &= b\left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor + n - b\left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor \\
        i(b,n) &= n \\
        i(5,16) &= 16 
      \end{aligned} 
    \end{equation}

    \chapter{The Slants} \label{slants}
    A \textbf{slant} is a special kind of set of cells. We shall define a slant\footnote{We can also use the word `slope', but some readers may confuse it with the mathematical definition of a slope.} with these traits:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item None of the cells in the set belong in the same row
        \item None of the cells in the set belong in the same column
        \item Only one slant of its kind has $b$ number of cells; it is the longest slant
        \item Unlike rows and columns, different slants have different number of cells
        \item Two kinds of slants exist, the \textbf{diagonal} and \textbf{antidiagonal} slants
    \end{itemize}

    In a square grid, there are $2b-1$ number of slants of each kind in a square grid. For example, if the square has a base of 5, there shall be 9 slants for each kind. Let $l$ be a function that takes $b$ as an argument to determine the number of slants in a square.
    \begin{equation}
        \begin{split}
            l(b) &= 2b - 1 \\
            l(5) &= 2(5) - 1 \\
            l(5) &= 9
        \end{split}
    \end{equation}

    \section{The Intersection Sum} \label{intersection_sum}
    There are equations that determine the slant a cell belongs to.

    By adding the row and the column index of a cell, we do an \textbf{intersection sum}. If two cells have the same intersection sums, they belong in the same antidiagonal slant. Let $\sigma$ be the intersection sum function and $n$ be the cell index number.
    \begin{equation}
        \begin{split}
            \sigma(b,n) &= r(b,n) + c(b,n) \\
            \operatorname{r}(b,n) &= \left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor + 1 \\
            \operatorname{c}(b,n) &= (n\bmod b) + 1 \\
            \sigma(b,n) &= ( \left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor + 1 ) + ((n \bmod b) + 1)\\
            \sigma(b,n) &= \left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor + (n \bmod b) + 2\\
        \end{split}
    \end{equation}

    The intersection sum is crucial to finding all the antidiagonal slants in a square grid.

    \section{The Intersection Difference} \label{intersection_diff}
    To do an \textbf{intersection difference}, we shall subtract the column index of a cell from the row index of the same cell. If two cells have the same intersection difference, they belong in the same diagonal slant. Let $\delta$ be the intersection difference function and $n$ be the cell index number.
    \begin{equation}
        \begin{split}
            \delta(b,n) &= r(b,n) - c(b,n)\\
            \operatorname{r}(b,n) &= \left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor + 1 \\
            \operatorname{c}(b,n) &= (n\bmod b) + 1 \\
            \delta(b,n) &= (\left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor + 1) - ((n\bmod b) + 1)\\
                        &= \left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor + 1 - (n\bmod b) - 1\\
            \delta(b,n) &= \left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor - (n\bmod b)\\
        \end{split}
    \end{equation}

    The intersection difference is crucial to finding all the descending slants in a square grid.

        \item If $\delta(b,n) < 0$, it is a superdiagonal cell.
        \item If $\delta(b,n) = 0$, it belongs to the main diagonal.

    \section{The Cardinality of a Given Slant} \label{slant_cardinality}
    Not all slants have the same number of cells. Only $b-1$ pairs of slants of have the same number of cells for each kind of slants. To get the number of cells in a given slant index. Let $x$ be either a diagonal ($d$) or an antidiagonal ($a$) index. Let $X$ be a set of cells belonging to a chosen slant. The vertical bar notation ($|A|$) denotes the \textbf{cardinality} of a set (\cite{math_vault}). However, the vertical bar notation can also denote an absolute number; we shall use $n(X)$ to denote the cardinality of set $X$. We shall use the vertical bars for denoting absolute values.
    \begin{equation}
        n(X) = b - |b - x|
    \end{equation}
    Only slants with index $b$ have the most number of cells which has a count of $b$.

    \section{The Letter L and Capital Gamma} \label{u_and_w}
    Function $f$ shall denote any function in this subsection. Set $X$ shall denote any set in this subsection. Function $f$ will surely cover all the cells of the first column and either the first or the last row.
    \begin{equation}\
            X = \{ f(b,x) : x \in \mathbb{N}, 0 \leq x \leq 2b \}
    \end{equation}
    The range of the any set in this subsection has these traits:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item When $x = b$ then $f(b,x) = 0$. This covers the first column
        \item When $1 \leq x \leq b$ then $f(b,x) = b^2 - bx$. This may cover the first or the last row.
    \end{itemize}
    Function $\operatorname{u}$ is a function that either yields a multiple of $b$ or subtracts $b$ from $x$ depending on whether $x$ exceeds or fall short of $b$. This forms an capital gamma-shape ($\Gamma$) in the square matrix as it covers the first column then the first row.
    \begin{equation}
        u(b,x) = b^{2 + \left\lfloor -\frac{x}{b} \right\rfloor }|b-x|
    \end{equation}
    \begin{equation}
        U = \{ u(b,x) : x \in \mathbb{N}, 1 \leq x \leq 2b - 1 \}
    \end{equation}
    The range of set $U$ has these own traits:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item When $x < b$ then $u(b,x)$ will be a multiple of $b$ from $0$ to $b^2-b$
        \item When $x \geq b$ then $u(b,x) = |b - x|$
    \end{itemize}

    Function $\operatorname{w}$ also works like function $\operatorname{u}$ but it rather forms an L-shape as it covers the first column then the last row.
    \begin{equation}
      w(b,x) = (1 - \left\lfloor \frac{x}{b} \right\rfloor)(b|b-x|) + (\left\lfloor \frac{x}{b} \right\rfloor)(x - b)
    \end{equation}
    \begin{equation}
        W = \{ w(b,x) : x \in \mathbb{N}, 1 \leq x \leq 2b - 1 \}
    \end{equation}
    The range of set $W$ has these own traits:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item When $x < b$ then $w(b,x)$ will be a multiple of $b$ from $0$ to $b^2-b$
        \item When $x \geq b$ then $w(b,x) = b^2 - |b - x|$
    \end{itemize}

    \section{The Cells of a Slant} \label{cells_of_a_slant}
    We will use several notations to describe the different kind of slants and their cells.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item A diagonal slant is denoted as $d_{x,*}b$ where $x$ is the index of the diagonal slant. This can be also called as ``diagonal slant x"
        \item A cell in a diagonal slant is denoted as $d_{x,o}b$ where $o$ is the nth cell of diagonal slant x.
        \item To depict the nth cell of each diagonal slant, we will denote it as $d_{*,o}b$. (eg. All the 1st cells of each diagonal slant in 5-square is denoted as $d_{*,1}5$.)
        \item An antidiagonal slant is denoted as $d'_{x,*}b$ where $x$ is the index of the antidiagonal slant. This can be also called as ``ascending slant x"
        \item A cell in an antidiagonal slant is denoted as $d'_{x,o}b$ where $o$ is the nth cell of antidiagonal slant x.
        \item To depict the nth cells of each antidiagonal slants we will denote it as $d'_{*,o}b$. (eg. All the 1st cells of each antidiagonal slant in 5-square is denoted as $d'_{*,1}5$.)
    \end{itemize}

    Before enumerating all the cells of a chosen slant, we must first find the cardinality of the chosen slant (see Section \ref{slant_cardinality}). The variable $s$ represents either a diagonal slant $d$ or an antidiagonal slant $d'$
    \begin{equation}
        n(s_{x,*}b) = b - |b-x|
    \end{equation}


    To enumerate the cells of a diagonal slant, we shall add the function used for enumerating the cell indices of the Main Diagonal (see \ref{diagonal}) and function $\operatorname{u}$ from section \ref{u_and_w}.
    \begin{equation}
        \begin{split}
          d_{x,o}b &= (b+1)(o-1) + u(b,x) \\
                &= (b+1)(o-1) + (b^{2 + \left\lfloor -\frac{x}{b} \right\rfloor }|b-x|)
        \end{split}
    \end{equation}

    We shall use the set builder notation to enumerate the cells. Variables $b$ and $x$ will be constant in this equation.
    \begin{equation}
        K = \{ d_{x,o}b : 1 \leq o \leq n(d_{x,*}b) \}
    \end{equation}

    To enumerate the cells of an ascending slant, we shall subtract the results of function $w$ (see Section \ref{u_and_w}) from the results of the function $\operatorname{A}$ that enumerates the anti-diagonal cells (see Section \ref{antidiagonal}).
    \begin{equation}
        \begin{split}
            d'_{x,o}b &= A(b,o) - w(b,x) \\
                &= (b^2 - bo + o - 1) + (1 - \left\lfloor \frac{x}{b} \right\rfloor)(b|b-x|) + (\left\lfloor \frac{x}{b} \right\rfloor)(x - b)
        \end{split}
    \end{equation}

    We shall use the set builder notation to enumerate the cells. Variables $b$ and $x$ will be constant in this equation.
    \begin{equation}
        L = \{ d'_{x,o}b : 1 \leq o \leq n(d'_{x,*}b) \}
    \end{equation}

    \chapter{Corners and Edges} \label{corners_and_edges}
    The corners and edges define the boundaries of a square. In geometry, a vertex is a point where two lines meet. In this section, we will liken a corner to a vertex; a cell belongs to two groups of cells called edges which we will liken to a side of a polygon. 

    \section{The Edges} \label{edges}
    An \textbf{edge} can either be a row or a column but not both. A corner belongs to a horizontal edge and a vertical edge; those are the two kinds of edges. A horizontal edge is a row, and a vertical edge is a column. For each kind of edge, there are two instances; in total, there are four edges in a square. The four edges have their respective names: top edge, bottom edge, left edge, right edge.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item The \textbf{top edge} is a horizontal edge for it is a row; it has an index of 1. The indices of its cells are always a series of natural numbers from 0 to $b - 1$.
        \item The \textbf{bottom edge} is a horizontal edge for it is a row; it has an index of $b$. The indices of its cells belong to a set of natural numbers that satisfies $b^2 - b \leq n \leq b^2 - 1$.
        \item The \textbf{left edge} is a vertical edge for it is a column; it has an index of 1. The indices of its cells belong to the range of the following set: $\{n : b(n-1), n = 1,\dots,b\}$.
        \item The \textbf{right edge} is a vertical edge for it is a column; it has an index of $b$. The indices of its cells belong to the range of the following set: $\{n : bn-1, n = 1,\dots,b\}$.
    \end{itemize}

    For example, in a 7-square, $a_{1,*}$ is the top edge, $a_{7,*}$ is the bottom edge, $a_{*,1}$ is the left edge, and $a_{*,7}$ is the right edge.

    \section{The Corners} \label{corners}
    A \textbf{corner} is where two edges meet: an intersection of two edges. These edges are not of the same kind; a corner is an intersection of a horizontal edge and a vertical edge. There are four corners in a square. The edges where they belong define their name.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item The \textbf{top-left corner} belongs to the top edge and left edge. It signifies the start of cell indices in a series. The cell index of a top-left corner is always 0, and its matrix index is always $a_{1,1}$.
        \item The \textbf{top-right corner} belongs to the top edge and right edge. The cell index of the top-right corner is always $b - 1$, and its matrix index is $a_{1,b}$.
        \item The \textbf{bottom-left corner} belongs to the bottom edge and left edge. The cell index of the bottom-left corner is always $b^2 - b$, and its matrix index is $a_{b,1}$.
        \item The \textbf{bottom-right corner} belongs to the bottom edge and right edge. It signifies the end of cell indices in a series. The cell index of the bottom-right corner is always $b^2 - 1$, and its matrix index is $a_{b,b}$.
    \end{itemize}

    For example, in a 4-square, $a_{1,1}$ or $i_0$ is the top-left corner, $a_{1,4}$ or $i_3$ is the top-right corner, $a_{4,1}$ or $i_{12}$ is the bottom-left corner, and $a_{4,4}$ or $i_{15}$ is the bottom-right corner.

    \chapter{The Traversal between Cells} \label{traversal}
    Traversing the grid means choosing a \textbf{starting point} and choosing the \textbf{adjacent cell} to move to. Depending on the starting point, the direction of the movement can be left or right, up-down, or a mix of both: ascending or descending diagonally or antidiagonally.
    
    \section{Horizontal Steps} \label{h_steps}
    A \textbf{horizontal step} is the simplest one. Let $n$ be the starting point and $x$ be the adjacent point. To step to the right. We simply do
    \begin{equation}
      x = n + 1
    \end{equation}
    However, there are edges (see Section \ref{edges}). Even though the cell indices can be treated as a modulo group of $b^2$, we can still establish the borders; in this case we check for the right edge. We will simply add the following equation to our right step.
    \begin{equation}
      x = n + 1 - \left\lfloor \frac{(n \bmod b) + 1}{b} \right\rfloor
    \end{equation}
    When $n + 1 < b - 1$, a right step can still be done; otherwise it will stay still. The part $(n \bmod b) + 1$ is the function $\operatorname{c}$ we used for getting the column of a cell (see Chapter \ref{columns}).


    To step to the left, we simply do
    \begin{equation}
      x = n - 1
    \end{equation}
    \newpage
    And to check for the left edge, we do
    \begin{equation}
      x = n - \left\lceil \frac{((n \bmod b) + 1)-1}{b} \right\rceil \\
        = n - \left\lceil \frac{n \bmod b}{b} \right\rceil
    \end{equation}

    \section{Vertical Steps} \label{v_steps}
    Like the horizontal steps, a \textbf{vertical step} is also simple. The movement directions are down and up. To move down, we simply do  
    \begin{equation} 
      x = n + b
    \end{equation}
To check if we hit the bottom edge, we do
    \begin{equation}
      x = n + b - b\left\lfloor \frac{\left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor+1}{b} \right\rfloor
    \end{equation}
The function $\operatorname{r}$ or this part $\left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor+1$ is used to check if we hit the bottom edge, the last row (see Chapter \ref{rows}).


To move up, we simply do
    \begin{equation} 
      x = n + b
    \end{equation}
To check the top edge, the first row, we do
    \begin{equation}
      x = n + b\left\lceil \frac{(\left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor + 1) - 1}{b} \right\rceil \\
        = n + b\left\lceil \frac{\left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor}{b} \right\rceil
    \end{equation}

    \section{Steps Along the Slants} \label{slant_steps}
    A horizontal step means incrementing or decrementing the starting cell's column index by 1. As for the vertical step, the row index is changed instead. A slant step is basically a combination of a horizontal step and a vertical step where the adjacent cell is in the same slant as the starting point. There are four kinds of slant steps. Let $x$ be the adjacent cell, $n$ be the starting point, and $r$ and $c$ be the row and column index of $n$.
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Diagonal descension} is a step along a diagonal slant. It is a down-right step. It is expressed as $x = n + (b + 1)$. To ensure it is stopped at either the right edge or bottom edge, we do $x = n + (b - b\left\lfloor \frac{r}{b} \right\rfloor) + (1 - \left\lfloor \frac{c}{b} \right\rfloor)$. 
    \item \textbf{Diagonal ascension} is a step along a diagonal slant. It is an up-left step. It is expressed as $x = n - (b + 1)$. To ensure it is stopped at either the left edge or top edge, we do $x = n + b\left\lceil \frac{r-1}{b} \right\rceil - \left\lceil \frac{c-1}{b} \right\rceil$. 
    \item \textbf{Antidiagonal ascension} is a step along an antidiagonal slant. It is an up-right step. It is expressed as $x = n - (b - 1)$. To ensure it is stopped at either the right edge or top edge, we do $x = n + b\left\lceil \frac{r-1}{b} \right\rceil + (1 - \left\lfloor \frac{c}{b} \right\rfloor)$. 
    \item \textbf{Antidiagonal descension} is a step along an antidiagonal slant. It is a down-left step. It is expressed as $x = n + (b - 1)$. To ensure it is stopped at either the left edge or bottom edge, we do $x = n + (b - b\left\lfloor \frac{r}{b} \right\rfloor) + \left\lceil \frac{c-1}{b} \right\rceil$. 

  \end{itemize}

    \chapter{The Symmetry} \label{symmetry}
    A square is always symmetrical, bilaterally and quadrilaterally. Every symmetrical thing has a center. When treated as a finite tuple, rows and columns have one or two center cells depending on whether their length is odd or even.

    \section{The Center Index} \label{center_index}
    A \textbf{center index} pertains to a term or a pair of terms in the middle of a tuple.
    \begin{equation*}
        T = (1,2,3,4,5)
    \end{equation*}
    The value of the terms of tuple $T$ equate to their respective indices. The center index is 3. To find the center index of a tuple will use a function that can be interpreted in two ways. Let $x$ be the length of the tuple or any natural number.
    \begin{equation}
        M(x) = \left\lceil \frac{x}{2} \right\rceil = \left\lfloor \frac{x+1}{2} \right\rfloor
    \end{equation}
    We will define the result of $M(x)$ as the center index. 

    When the tuple has an even number of terms, the center indices are two: the \textbf{main center index} and the \textbf{secondary center index}.
    \begin{equation*}
        S = (1,2,3,4,5,6)
    \end{equation*}
    Tuple $S$ is similar to tuple $T$ but with one more term. Tuple $S$ has an even number of terms, therefore it has two center indices. The main center index is 3 and the secondary center index is 4. The secondary center index is always equal to $M(x) + 1$ since it follows the main center index. From another perspective, in an tuple of odd number of terms, the main and secondary center index are the same. We will use this function that can work on any tuple, be their number of terms be odd or even. This function will return the secondary center index.
    \begin{equation} \label{even_center_indices}
      \begin{split}
        M_{2}(x)  &= \left\lceil \frac{x}{2} \right\rceil + 1 - x\bmod 2 = \left\lfloor \frac{x+1}{2} \right\rfloor + 1 - x\bmod 2 \\
                  &= M(x) + 1 - x\bmod 2
      \end{split}
    \end{equation}

    Functions $M$ and $M_2$ applicable to rows and columns when they are treated as tuples.

    \section{Center of the Base} \label{center_base}
    The base is the very core of a square and always a variable in our equations so far. To find the center of the base, we will use the function for finding the center index of a tuple.
    \begin{equation}
        \begin{split}
            M(b) = \left\lceil \frac{b}{2} \right\rceil = \left\lfloor \frac{b+1}{2} \right\rfloor \\
            M_{2}(b) = M(b) + 1 - b\bmod 2
        \end{split}
    \end{equation}
    When $b$ is even, $M_{2}(b)$ is the second center index of the base. For example, when $b = 8$, $M(8) = 4$ and $M_{2}(8) = 5$. They denote the first center index and the secondary center index respectively.


    The center of the base is important for finding the center of a square.

    \section{Center of a Row and a Column} \label{center_row_column}
    A row and a column always has a $b$ number of cells. Using the intersection function (see Chapter \ref{intersection}), we will find the center of a row or a column. We will call the cell in the center of a row or column the \textbf{center cell}.

    To find the cell index of the center of a row, we must first determine the value of $M(b)$. For example, we will find the center of row 2 of a 5-square. Let $c$ be equal to the center of the base and $r$ be the chosen row. 
    \begin{equation}
        \begin{split}
            c &= M(b) = M(5) = \left\lceil \frac{5}{2} \right\rceil \\
            c &= 3 \\
            \operatorname{i}(b,r,c) &= b(r-1)+(c-1) \\
            \operatorname{i}(5,2,3) &= 5(2-1)+(3-1) \\
                &= 5 + 2 \\
            \operatorname{i}(5,2,3) &= 7 \\

We will call the squares with an odd base an \textbf{odd-base square}, and the squares with an even base is a \textbf{even-base square}.
    For example, to find the center cell of a 5-square, an odd-base square, we will use two ways. Let $m$ be the function to find the center cell of a square. This is the first way.

    \chapter{The Opposites}
    Each cell has its own \textbf{opposite cell} along its respective row, column, descending slant, or ascending slant. In a symmetry there are always two opposite ends.

    \section{The Opposite Index}
    An \textbf{opposite index} pertains to opposite term of a chosen term across a finite tuple of natural numbers.
    \begin{equation*}
        T = (1,2,3,4,5)
    \end{equation*}
    In this example, the value of the terms of tuple $T$ equate to their respective indices. The opposite index of 1 is 5, of 2 is 4, and of 3 is itself; in a tuple with an odd cardinality, there is always one term whose opposite index is itself: the center index (see Section \ref{center_index}). In a tuple of an even cardinality, all of the terms have their respective opposite index unequal to themselves. To find the opposite index of a term, we will use function $o$. Let $x$ be the chosen number and $l$ be the cardinality or length of the tuple.
    \begin{equation}
        o(l,x) = l + 1 - x
    \end{equation}
    We will apply this function to all the terms of tuple $T$. We will use a set builder on set $O$. Variable $l$ is equal to $n(T)$ or the cardinality of tuple $T$
    \begin{equation*}
        O = \{ o(x,l) : 1 \leq x \leq l\}
    \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*}
        \begin{split}
            o(l,x) &= l - 1 - x \\
            o(5,1) &= 5 + 1 - 1 = 5 \\
            o(5,2) &= 5 + 1 - 2 = 4 \\
            o(5,3) &= 5 + 1 - 3 = 3 \\
            o(5,4) &= 5 + 1 - 4 = 2 \\
            o(5,5) &= 5 + 1 - 5 = 1 \\
            O &= \{5,4,3,2,1\}
        \end{split}
    \end{equation*}
    Set $O$ is the range whose elements are the corresponding opposite indices of each term.

    \section{The Horizontal Opposite} \label{horizontal_opposite}

We will find the \textbf{diagonal opposite} of Cell 6 ($i_{6}$) in a 5-square. We will omit $(b,n)$ until it is needed to be shown.
    \begin{equation}
      \begin{split}
        r &= \left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor + 1 \\
        o_r &= b + 1 - \left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor + 1 \\
        o_r &= b - \left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor \\
        c &= n - b\left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor + 1 \\
        o_c &= b + 1 - (n - b\left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor + 1) \\
            &= b + 1 - n + b\left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor - 1) \\
        o_c &= b - n + b\left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor \\
        i(b,r,c) &= b(r-1)+(c-1) \\
        i(b,o_r,o_c) &= b((b - \left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor) - 1)+((b - n + b\left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor) -1) \\
                &= b(b - \left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor - 1)+(b - n + b\left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor -1) \\
                &= b^2 - b\left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor - b + b - n + b\left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor -1 \\
        \operatorname{d}(n,b) &= b^2 - n - 1 \\
        \operatorname{d}(5,16) &= 25 - 16 - 1 \\ 
        \operatorname{d}(5,16) &= 9 - 1 \\

    \section{The Diagonal Opposite}
    Of all the functions for finding an opposite of a cell, this one is the easiest. To find the \textbf{diagonal opposite} of a cell, we simply swap its row index with its column index. If its row index is equal to its column index, or $r=c$, the cardinality of its ascending slant is odd. However, in this Section, we will the do row-column swap while assigning value to neither $r$ nor $c$. A new function will be made from modifying the Intersection function $\operatorname{i}(b,r,n)$ We will find the ascending index of Cell 16 ($i_{16}$). Let $n$ be the chosen cell index.
    \begin{equation}
      \begin{split}
        r &= \left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor + 1 \\
        o_r &= b + 1 - \left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor + 1 \\
        o_r &= b - \left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor \\
        c &= n - b\left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor + 1 \\
        o_c &= b + 1 - (n - b\left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor + 1) \\
            &= b + 1 - n + b\left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor - 1) \\
        o_c &= b - n + b\left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor \\
        i(b,r,c) &= b(r-1)+(c-1) \\
        i(b,o_r,o_c) &= b((b - \left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor) - 1)+((b - n + b\left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor) -1) \\
                &= b(b - \left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor - 1)+(b - n + b\left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor -1) \\
                &= b^2 - b\left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor - b + b - n + b\left\lfloor \frac{n}{b} \right\rfloor -1 \\
        \operatorname{d}(n,b) &= b^2 - n - 1 \\
        \operatorname{d}(5,16) &= 25 - 16 - 1 \\ 
        \operatorname{d}(5,16) &= 9 - 1 \\ 
        \operatorname{d}(5,16) &= 8 \\ 
      \end{split} 
    \end{equation}
    The antidiagonal opposite of Cell 16 ($i_{17}$) is Cell 8 ($i_{8}$).
